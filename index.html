A calmer way through the first month.

Moving to another country to study means many small, urgent tasks, each with its own office, documents and deadline. I designed an app that keeps them in one place and always shows what to do next.

The project. Settle In is a concept I made to practise end-to-end product design: framing a problem, drawing flows and wireframes, building a small design system, and testing a clickable prototype.

My role. Everything on this page: the problem framing, the screens, the design system and the working prototype.

This is a self-initiated concept, not a client project. The people, places and numbers inside the screens are made up, and I have not run user research yet. Where this page describes users, it says whether it is an assumption.

The first weeks are scattered.

As a student who moved to India myself, I know that the practical tasks of arriving do not come in one list. They arrive as emails, group chats and notice boards, and it is not obvious which task is urgent.

Assumptions to test.

  • Students often do not know every task they must finish, or in what order.
  • Documents are needed for several tasks, so missing one blocks others.
  • Students read on small screens, sometimes in a second or third language.
  • A short, trusted list with a visible next step lowers stress.

These are my assumptions from personal experience, not research findings. The testing plan below shows how I would check them.

Who it is for.

A fictional persona, written from the assumptions above to keep design decisions focused.

Amara

First-year student, newly arrived from abroad. A made-up persona.

Goals

  • Finish the legal and practical basics early
  • Not miss a deadline
  • Feel less alone with it

Frustrations (assumed)

  • Information in many places
  • Unsure which documents to bring
  • Offices with long waits

Context (assumed)

  • Android phone, mostly one-handed
  • Patchy data in the hostel
  • English is her second language

Five rules for every screen.

  1. 1

    Show one next step

    A first month has dozens of tasks. The home screen always says what comes next, so nobody has to plan.

  2. 2

    Plain words, no jargon

    Short sentences and everyday words, because many students read in a second or third language.

  3. 3

    Honest about what we do not know

    Rules differ by university and bank. Every task says it is sample guidance and points to the international office.

  4. 4

    Easy to use with one hand

    Main actions sit in the lower half of the screen, and every tap target is at least 44 pixels tall.

  5. 5

    Never rely on colour alone

    Done tasks show a check and the word Done. Warnings use words and icons as well as colour.

Proposed measures of success once the app is tested: how many testers finish the bank task without help, how long they take to find the next task, and how many tasks they mark done in the first week. None of these has been measured yet.

The main path in six steps.

  1. WelcomePick a city and university
  2. Home checklistSee progress and what is next
  3. Task detailsDocuments and steps for one task
  4. DocumentsWhat is ready, what is missing
  5. NearbyPlaces that match the task
  6. HelpWho to call, including 112

Marking a task done updates the progress on the home screen straight away, and can be undone.

The Help tab is one tap from anywhere, because the moments when someone needs it are the moments they cannot search.

Accessibility decisions.

  • Readable text. Every text and background pair is at least 4.5:1, and checked by script.
  • Big tap targets. Buttons and rows are at least 44 px tall, and the tab bar items are 50 px.
  • Not only colour. Done tasks show a check mark and the word Done. Needed documents say Needed.
  • Focus follows the screen. When the screen changes, keyboard and screen reader focus moves to its title, and a polite announcement says where you are.
  • Plain language. Short sentences, no jargon, and numbers such as 112 stated clearly.
  • Motion on request. Animations stop when the device asks for reduced motion.
  • Honest content. Sample data is labelled as sample, so nobody mistakes it for advice.

How I would test it.

Not yet run. This is the plan for five international students, each in a 20-minute session using the prototype on a phone.

Tasks to give

  1. You just arrived. What would you do first?
  2. Find what to bring to open a bank account.
  3. Mark that task done. How many tasks are left?
  4. Something is wrong. Where do you look for help?

What I would watch

  • Can they finish each task without help?
  • Where do they hesitate or tap the wrong thing?
  • Do they understand the progress card?
  • Which words confuse them?

What I would do next.

Run the tests above and fix what they show, before adding anything new.

Get the content checked by a university international office, because the checklist is only as good as its facts.

Support more languages. The layout leaves room for longer words, so translated versions would not break it.

Work with poor connections. The checklist should open and update without data, and sync later.
